01 The starting point

Cities are asked to deliver the future — but rarely allowed to imagine it.

The biggest constraint in urban transformation is not technical. It is institutional — and it has three faces: a missing mandate, a mounting urgency, and a shared capacity to act that is quietly thinning out.

Cities have no mandate to imagine the future

Thinking about the future is, almost nowhere, in a planner’s job description. Planning law is built to administer — check, zone, permit, archive — not to test and choose between futures. “The urgent eclipses the important,” trapping cities in cycles of reaction.

The warning sign: Ireland now has so few planners that, since 2025, town planning is an official shortage occupation — 62% of authorities struggle to recruit and retain them.

Fracture 01 · Mandate

No mandate to imagine

Planning is built to administer — zone, permit, archive — never to picture and choose between futures.

Yet the pressure to transform has never been higher

Paris, the New Urban Agenda, the EU Green Deal, the European Climate Law, the Mission on Adaptation. Between 2013 and 2023, EEA member countries with a national adaptation strategy rose from 20 to 32, while Covenant of Mayors adaptation signatories grew from a few hundred to over 4,500.

The mandate to deliver the future keeps growing. The mandate to imagine it does not.

And the shared capacity to act together is thinning

In society, algorithmic curation has split the public into separate informational worlds and trust in institutions has eroded. The same fracture runs inside the municipality: departments operate as silos, budgets tighten, and staff are too busy operating the present to imagine the future.

Fracture 03 · Unity

A fraying common ground

Society splits into separate worlds; inside the institution, departments work in silos.

A mandate that is missing. An urgency that is mounting. A unity that is fraying.

This is the gap CoPlanAI is built for — the connective layer that lets a city, its institutions and its people imagine and choose a future in public, together.

02 The demand

What cities ask for — and what we build.

That gap reaches us as concrete requests — real ones, in clients’ own words. Beneath each is the use case we actually deliver, which is rarely the literal automation that was asked for.

Agents that synthesise, so people don’t transcribe

The hours we save are the manual ones: agents cluster, theme and summarise thousands of contributions. Officials review the synthesis instead of compiling it — the judgment stays human, the clerical work doesn’t.

Use case · synthesis

“We want to save hundreds of hours a month with AI.”

Agents represent the absent — they never replace the public

We tune agents to stand in for the people who can’t be in the room — children, future residents, the species a plan overlooks — and to stress-test scenarios. The living public’s voice is the one thing we will not automate away.

Use case · representation

“Can we replace participants with AI agents?”

A plan becomes options people can react to

A site plan turns into explorable, supervised scenarios — not one hero render to admire, but variations citizens and professionals can compare, contest and rate. The image becomes an instrument for a decision, not decoration for one already made.

Use case · visualisation

“Give us a site plan and turn it into renders.”

Deliberation at the scale of a whole population

The same generative loop runs online, between in-person sessions — reaching thousands who would never attend an evening workshop. Not a comment box: people propose, see consequences and converge, asynchronously, at city scale.

Use case · scale

“Automate the online participation process — we’d subscribe yearly.”

A documented mandate, not a comment count

Because every participant worked the same images and votes, the output is a ranked, visual, deliberated record of what a community can agree to want — defensible to a council, bankable to a developer, auditable later.

Use case · evidence

“Make our consultation results defensible to elected members.”

Policy pre-evaluation, while change is still cheap

Agents score each scenario against EU policy, the local design guide and city strategy as it is made — surfacing conflicts early, when an image is easy to change, instead of as a veto at the end of an expensive process.

03 Our position

Critique. Repair. Innovate.

Our stance toward AI in planning is neither refusal nor surrender — it is a working method.

Three moves, one method

Critique — name what automation misses. Fully automated processes optimise the existing mandate — permits, compliance, throughput — and inherit every blind spot of the system they accelerate.

Repair — put back what process lost: seeing consequences before deciding, and hearing the voices the process excludes.

Innovate — give cities a projective instrument: a way to make futures visible, debatable and choosable.

Our position

Critique · Repair · Innovate

Neither refusal nor surrender — a working method.

Where the three roles meet: coplanning

Stakeholders, institutions and AI agents each bring something different. Coplanning is the overlap where all three meet — and AI never holds the pen alone: every generative step is wrapped in a human step. Propose, then judge; render, then decide.

The output of a workshop is not a design. It is a mandate — documented evidence of what a community can agree to want, which professionals then translate into technical design.

04 Democracy

Democracy isn’t just about choice. It’s about rethinking your choice.

The method in three steps — Helsinki Summer Streets, with the City of Helsinki.

1 · Sense

January 2023. A photo of a real street — the only input citizens need to start.

2 · Decide

A citizen committee and entrepreneurs generate, debate and vote — in one room, around one table.

3 · Act

June 2023. The street as built — Helsinki Summer Streets, with the City of Helsinki.

The sequence we all know too well

The ballot-box model is the wrong template for design: you choose once, blind, and live with it. We have all seen the same sequence play out — participation, then discontent, then demolition. Conflict surfaces after the money is spent.

Participation — the experiment is launched and consulted on.
Discontent — residents sharply divided once it is built.
Demolition — the asphalt comes up again.

The pattern participatory AI is built to break.

The right we build for

To know consequences — and to change your choice in public. In Helsinki, first instincts (bike lanes, everywhere) evolved once people could compare consequences together. That evolution is the democratic act.

CoPlanAI · working principle

“The value of participation is not in deciding what most people want, but in discovering what we could want, together.”

The Hippocratic Oath of working with AI

Bringing AI into a planning organisation is not a tooling decision — it transforms the institution. So the institution owes itself an explicit ethic. Ours, in four commitments — and we ask every partner organisation to write their own.

The oath

Four commitments

  1. Augment judgment; never automate it away.
  2. Preserve every participant’s right to know consequences and change their choice.
  3. Make the model’s biases legible before asking anyone to trust its images.
  4. Represent those who cannot be in the room.

05 Repair

CoPlanAI as a reparative technology.

Chapter 01 named three fractures. CoPlanAI is built to mend all three — and the deepest repair is to participation itself, which has a default participant while everyone else is structurally absent.

One platform, three repairs

No mandate to imagine → a projective instrument. From a single photo, generate and compare real futures in public — imagination as a standing capacity, not a one-off exercise.

No capacity to match the urgency → the loop, compressed. Imagine, test and decide inside one workshop — what took survey cycles lands in days, with a documented mandate.

A fraying common ground → one shared visual table. Citizens, officials and departments work the same images and the same evidence at once.

The repair

From commentators to placemakers

Children 6–14. With an Architects’ Council of Europe research grant we built a child-specific participatory AI — drawing-guided for the youngest, tablet co-design for the oldest — piloted in Finnish schools with Tampere University and at Milan Digital Week.

Exploring climate futures with the young

UNDP Accelerator Labs Panama ran workshop series with several hundred participants from local high schools and universities — turning photographs of their own waterfront into climate-adaptation scenarios they could debate and own.

Parameters beyond people

People tend to think people-first — or people-only. At BiodiverseCity? (Estonian Museum of Natural History, Tallinn — European Green Capital 2023), a tuned model let children explore what European greening policies mean: communal areas reimagined as wilder, more biodiverse habitats.

Not a render to admire — a proposal to examine

Panama City, UNDP Accelerator Labs. Drag the handle: the site as participants photographed it, and the same site as the youth groups proposed it — a proposal to examine, contest and own.

The honest caveat — customisation is also a new bias

Every tuned agent over-represents some features and under-represents others. Choosing a bias is choosing whose city gets imagined more easily — so we treat bias curation as design work, done in the open.

Bias, curated in the open

Four practices

  1. Declare — every custom agent ships with a plain-language note on what it favours and what it flattens.
  2. Localise — models tuned with local imagery, policy and references — with the client, not for them.
  3. Counter-balance — reparative biases (children, elders, biodiversity) introduced openly, as policy choices.
  4. Supervise — professionals review outputs against regulation and quality before anything moves toward decision.

06 Regulation & quality

Imagination, evaluated against policy.

The newest layer answers the question every authority asks next: fine, but is it compliant — and is it good? Agents evaluate co-designed scenarios against EU policy, local design guides and municipal strategy, while professionals keep the final word.

Scored against EU policy and the local design guide

Bologna Verde, 2026 — with the Comune di Bologna, citizen scenarios for greener streets are evaluated by agents trained on EU policy and Bologna’s own catalogue of permeable, vegetal and mineral materials — nature-based solutions checked while imagination is still cheap to change.

The design guide becomes evaluation criteria

Architectural quality enters the loop as data, not as a veto at the end. The city’s own catalogue of materials and solutions is what the agents score against.

Testing a city model block by block

For the City of Vienna’s Supergrätzl programme, scenario evaluation helps planners and residents see how the model lands on specific streets — cooler, calmer, more playable — before commitment, in the city’s own visual language. Also in evaluation practice: Dubai Municipality, with planning law in the loop.

07 Evidence

Where CoPlanAI is already in use.

More than 90 projects in over 25 countries — each a different method, scale and institutional setting. A selection:

Trust as infrastructure

Munich · Zamanand Festival. 3,000+ ideas submitted, ~300 votes cast on reimagining the public space around the Siegestor — a festival-led experiment in rebuilding trust between city and citizens through social imagination.

Residents design a future park

Dubai Municipality · UAE. Co-planning sessions where residents shaped an interactive play area and shaded cycling track — alongside our work on compliance-aware generation with planning law in the loop.

A city-centre vision, co-planned

Lahti · Finland. A co-planning process feeding directly into the municipal strategy document — collective imagination given formal standing in the city’s most official text.

Co-designing a capital city

Nusantara · Indonesia. Local communities co-designing the future capital — participatory AI at the most consequential scale there is: a city that does not exist yet.

Regenerating Biloku, together

Pristina · Kosovo. A UNDP-led regeneration of a housing complex’s public spaces, with the mayor’s office at the table — participatory AI in a post-conflict planning context.

Reimagining vacant spaces

East Cleveland · USA. Seventh Hill’s Connect East Cleveland initiative — open-house co-design for vacant lots in a US disinvestment context, community-led from the first photograph.

Also: Helsinki (EU Public Sector Tech Watch Best Case Award) · Zürich · Berlin · Tallinn · World Government Summit · HafenCity University Hamburg.

The hard problems agencies bring us

Run one process that holds every stakeholder — a single workshop, in person and online at once: deep in the room, wide across the population.

Keep it compliant and well-designed — tune agents on regulation and design guides, run supervised workshops; accountable humans always make the call.

Make participation actually mean something — people propose, see consequences and converge, instead of just submitting opinions.

Go faster without faster rubber-stamps — front-load agreement; the expensive conflict moves to the cheap end.

The path — from first call to a mandate

Every engagement follows the same arc, packed into roughly two weeks: framing → capacity building → workshop set-up → workshops (in person + online) → supervision → mandate & handover.

What the office keeps: a documented mandate, a configured platform it can re-run on its own, and a trained team.

What you bring to get started

Starting is light — three things. One real question: a live planning or participation question worth piloting. A place or a plan: an existing site, or a future scenario to test. The people: a workshop lead from your team, plus the participants you want in the room — and the wider public online.

You bring

Three things

For participants: one simple, repeating loop

No plans to read, no software to learn. Start from a photo of the actual street. Propose in plain words — “more trees, a safer crossing.” See it rendered in seconds. Debate and compare variations side by side. Rate, converge — and every step is logged. One full loop takes about half a day, drawn from a sixteen-format method library.

In the room · online

One loop, about half a day — deep in the room, wide online.

For organisers: source & transform

Behind the room you stay in control. Pick where images come from — upload, library or map (phone shots keep their location) — and how participants reshape them: theme, draw or prompt.

Set the themes

Define the directions on the table — each with its own prompt, short description and expected impacts. Aim the agents at local context and planning rules, or let them imagine boldly.

Steer it live

During the session, a live gallery with built-in impact assessment lets you moderate the discussion and watch ideas land in real time.

10 Next step · governance innovation

The next step isn’t a tool. It’s governance innovation.

Working with CoPlanAI is not buying software. It is building a governance capability — the standing ability to convene every actor around one shared process.

Own the connective layer

A planning decision pulls on the same actors almost everywhere — developers, the municipality, experts, citizens, businesses, politicians — yet they rarely sit at one table, and the public is usually consulted last. The opportunity is not another plan. It is the connective layer between the actors. No one owns it by default; whoever convenes it sets the standard for everyone else.

The stakeholder linkage model

One table, not a sequence

Developers · municipality · experts · citizens · businesses · politicians.

What a shared process establishes

One table, not a sequence — the actors in the same visual conversation before positions harden. A repeatable participation standard — a method any private plan can be asked to run. Evidence everyone trusts — because every actor worked the same images and votes.

Linkage · process · mandate
